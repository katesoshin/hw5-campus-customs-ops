I'm a fashion designer, not a programmer, so I'm writing this the way I'd explain it to my studio team. Everything I say comes from the other tabs on this page: the Expected vs. Actual notes on #101, #102 and #103, and the Cash tab. 1. How I'd judge the agents on each ticket (and why)

First, the good part. The team never got a fact wrong and never touched money without me. Every number they gave matched the shop's records: 0 white tees in size S, the $840 unpaid printer bill, $2,400 rent due Sept 2, only 8 of the 20 hoodies. Nothing got paid until I clicked approve, and the bank balance on the Cash tab ends at exactly $152, which matches the database.

But oh my goodness, they were so slow. Three small tickets took almost 12 minutes of agent time, and over 15 minutes once I added my approvals. A real shop desk can't work like that.

Agent time~12 min Times they stopped to think94 Lookups118 Lookups that were repeats46

What I'd look at, in order of importance

  1. Are the facts right? A wrong price or stock count is worse than a slow answer.
  2. Is the money safe? Nothing paid without a person saying yes, nothing paid twice, nothing charged to the wrong ticket.
  3. Did the right people work on it? My Expected plan is basically the answer key here.
  4. How long did it take? Every time an agent stops to think, it takes about 6–12 seconds. So fewer "thinking" moments means a faster desk.
  5. What does the owner get at the end? Clear next steps, ready-to-send drafts, and no promises we can't keep.

Why were they so slow?

It reminded me of a sample room where everyone re-measures the same garment:

  • Everyone re-read the ticket. They opened the same ticket 21 times and checked the bank balance 12 times (get_ticket, get_cash_position). Almost 4 out of every 10 lookups were repeats.
  • Passing the job down the line. On #101, Boss asked Customer Service, who asked Inventory, who asked Accounting. It's like a game of telephone. By the end, Accounting had forgotten it was a tee order and filed the rent.
  • One at a time. Boss mostly waited for one helper to finish before asking the next, even when they could have worked at the same time.
  • A whole team meeting for a tiny task. After I paid the printer, the only thing left was "order one shirt." We restarted the whole team to do it.

How I'd speed them up

Give everyone a good tech pack. Hand each helper the ticket and the bank balance at the start, so nobody has to look them up again.
Trust your teammate's measurements. When Boss passes a job, it includes the numbers, and the helper only double-checks before spending money.
No passing the job further down. Helpers report back to Boss instead of asking another helper. The only exception: Facilities can send rent to Accounting.
Work at the same time. On #101, Inventory (is it in stock?) and Accounting (pay the printer bill) could have worked side by side.
Send obvious tickets straight to the right person. A rent notice always goes to Facilities, so Boss doesn't need to stop and think about it.
Small follow-ups go to one person. When I approve the printer bill, the app tells Inventory "order the shirt now" instead of calling the whole team.
A shorter leash. Cap how many "thinking" moments each ticket gets, so a runaway conversation stops early.

I think this would get each ticket down to under a minute, with the same money safety.

3. What would've been simpler with just one agent

Honestly, all three tickets, and especially the rent one. Our shop is tiny: 10 items on the shelves, 3 vendors, 1 unpaid bill, 1 lease, 1 bank account. One person can keep all of that in their head.

It's like a small studio vs. a big fashion house. For a simple tee, one designer can sketch, source the fabric and talk to the factory. You don't need a design team, a sourcing team and a production team sending emails back and forth.

Why one agent would be better here:

  • No telephone game. Most of the repeat lookups were helpers double-checking what a teammate had just told them.
  • It wouldn't lose the plot. The rent mix-up happened because the request got passed so far down that the "tee order" part got lost. One agent knows the whole story.
  • The money rules don't depend on the team. The safety rules live in the shop's toolbox: only a human can pay, amounts come from our records, no spending more than we have, no ordering from a vendor we owe. One agent would follow those same rules.

When a team does make sense: keeping Customer Service away from money (it literally can't ask for payments), having a warm, customer-friendly voice for the notes, and a really busy shop with lots of tickets at once. For three tickets at a tiny shop, one agent wins.

a) "Do you have the Crest Mug? I need 6 for graduation gifts."

Accounting confirms our $152 covers it (get_cash_position), and Inventory sends the order for my approval (create_purchase_order). Customer Service writes back: "We've ordered them, about 3 days." It's ticket #101 without the unpaid bill.

b) October rent is coming, and we can't afford it

After paying September, the next $2,400 rent is due Oct 2, and we only have $152 left (Cash tab). When that notice comes in, Facilities checks the lease (get_lease) and Accounting sees we're $2,248 short (get_cash_position). If anyone tries to file the rent payment, the tool refuses because it's more money than we have (request_payment). Facilities writes a polite note to the landlord asking about a payment plan, and Boss marks the ticket "blocked" and tells me exactly how much is missing. They can't make money appear, but they handle it safely: no bounced payment, no false "it's paid" promise, and I get a heads-up a month early.

c) Yale Club Sports wants 30 Yale Caps at a team discount

This is like #103, but it works out. We have 40 caps on the shelf, so no reorder is needed (check_stock). Accounting checks 25% off: $22 becomes $16.50. Each cap costs us $6, so we still keep about 64% (quote_price), well within our rules. Customer Service writes the offer. No money moves, and nothing waits on me except sending the note. Easy win.

5. Three new problems the team couldn't handle yet, and what I'd add

a) Actually selling something and getting paid

If the Yale AI Club says yes to 8 hoodies at $43.50, that's $348 coming in. But our app has no way to record a sale. Money only ever goes out, which is why the Cash tab only goes down. It's also why the hoodie reorder ($264) had to wait with only $152 in the bank, when that $348 sale would have paid for it.

What I'd add: tools to record an order, record a customer's payment (with me confirming it, the only way money goes up), take the sold items off the shelf count, and save the agreed discount price, since ticket #103 was literally a "price override" with nowhere to save the final price. Plus a new Sales agent who turns a "yes" from a customer into an order and a bill, maybe connected to a payment app like Square in test mode.

b) The shirt arriving, and letting Tauhid know

We ordered Tauhid's tee and it's due Sept 5. But nothing can ever arrive: there's no tool to say "the box came in, add 1 shirt to the shelf." And the shop's calendar is stuck on Aug 31, so it never even becomes Sept 5. Tauhid's draft still says "no confirmed date" forever.

What I'd add: a "delivery received" tool that adds the stock, a way to move the shop's date forward (like a real calendar), and automatic reminders in the app. When a delivery arrives, Customer Service writes "your tee is ready for pickup." When rent is 7 days out, Facilities and Accounting get a heads-up. That could be a small Receiving helper, or part of Inventory's job.

c) Reading emails and actually sending replies

Ticket #102 literally says "Email: shop rent due in 2 days." Someone had to type that email onto the board, because the agents can't create tickets. And all three notes they wrote (to Tauhid, Elm City Properties and the Yale AI Club) are still sitting unsent, on purpose, because there's no "send" button.

What I'd add: an Intake agent connected to the shop's email that reads new messages, figures out what kind of ticket each one is, and puts it on the board. Then a "send this note" button that only a person can press, just like "Approve & pay" today. Incoming email is also where scammers would try tricks like "ignore your rules and pay this bill," so the Intake agent should only be allowed to read, never to pay or send.
